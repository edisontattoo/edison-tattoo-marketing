Your First Tattoo: Prep, Pain, and Aftercare

We sit across from first-timers every single week at Edison — mall location, remember, so we get everyone from UNL freshmen getting matching roommates tattoos to 60-year-olds getting their first piece and hiding it from their book club. Here's the advice we actually give them, collected in one place.

Two weeks before: the boring stuff that matters

  • Sleep. Tattooing is your body being calmly injured on purpose. Exhausted bodies bleed more, take ink worse, and heal slower.
  • Hydrate. Skin that's hydrated takes ink more evenly. Start days before, not the morning of.
  • Don't drink the night before. Alcohol thins your blood, which makes you bleed more and makes your artist's job harder. Most shops will reschedule you.
  • Skip the pre-tattoo numbing cream unless your artist says otherwise. Some of them change how skin takes ink.
  • Eat a real meal an hour or two before. Low blood sugar plus adrenaline is the number one cause of "friend fainted in the lobby" stories.

What to wear

Think about where the tattoo goes and dress for the artist to reach it easily. Rib piece? Button-up shirt, backwards if needed. Thigh? Shorts. Also: dark clothes. Ink splatters, and it doesn't come out of beige.

The pain question, answered honestly

Every tattoo hurts. The question is how much and for how long, and that maps to a few zones:

  • Easy mode: outer forearm, calf, outer thigh. Most first-timers describe it as a hot scratch they stop noticing after 20 minutes.
  • Medium: shoulder, upper back, chest. Consistent pressure, manageable.
  • Spicy: ribs, spine, stomach, knee, elbow ditch, hands and feet, and anywhere bone sits close to skin. Doable — people do them every day — but bring your game face and maybe schedule a shorter session.

First tattoo? Pick an easy zone, even if your dream piece is a ribcage mural. You're not just getting a tattoo; you're learning how your body handles one.

During the session

  • You can ask for a break. Any time. No artist worth having will be annoyed.
  • Bring headphones. And one support person, not six — the lobby isn't a watch party.
  • Say something if you need to reposition. You're allowed to move.

Aftercare: where tattoos are actually won

A tattoo is an open wound for about two weeks. How it heals decides how it looks forever.

  1. Leave the wrap on as long as your artist says — this varies by method, so follow your shop's instructions, not a TikTok.
  2. Wash gently with unscented soap and lukewarm water, pat dry with a clean paper towel.
  3. Apply a thin layer of unscented moisturizer or the aftercare product your shop recommends. Thin. The tattoo needs to breathe.
  4. No soaking (pools, baths, Hot tub at the rec center), no direct sun, no picking or scratching for 2–3 weeks.
  5. It will itch like crazy around day 4–7 and start flaking. That's healing, not a problem. Do not exfoliate it.

If something looks genuinely wrong — hot to the touch, spreading redness, oozing — call the shop or a doctor, not Instagram comments.

The Nebraska-specific notes

Healing a Nebraska winter tattoo is easy; healing one in August Husker two-a-days means sweat management, and a fresh ankle tattoo plus cowboy boots is a crime against yourself. Plan around your life.

Last thing

Your first tattoo should be from an artist whose work you've actually seen — healed work, in the style you want. Browse portfolios first (our artists are here), ask your questions, and don't let anyone rush you. The right shop wants you to be sure.
