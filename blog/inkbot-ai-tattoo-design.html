We Built an AI That Designs Your Tattoo: The Story of InkBot

There's a moment we watched happen at the counter every week, at our shop inside Gateway Mall in Lincoln. Someone walks in with the idea — "a moth, but half flowers, half clockwork" — and no way to show it. They scroll their phone. They find nothing. They either settle for something generic or they walk out, and the idea walks out with them.

We got tired of watching good ideas die at the "I can't explain it" stage. So we built a tool for our own website: an AI assistant called InkBot that lives at edisontattoo.net.

What InkBot actually does

It's not a tattoo printer. It's the conversation-starter we always wished existed:

  • Explore your idea. Describe the concept in plain words — InkBot helps you shape the imagery, style, and placement before you ever talk to an artist.
  • Answer the basic questions — placement, sizing, aftercare, how walk-ins work at our shop — any time of day, because inspiration at 2am deserves answers at 2am.
  • Get you to the right artist. Fine line, black and grey, ear projects: the tool helps route your idea to the human best suited to draw it.

What InkBot will never do

Here's the part we feel strongly about, because some shops are acting like AI replaces the artist. It doesn't, and it shouldn't.

AI can generate a picture of a tattoo. It can't:

  • Read your skin. How a design wraps a forearm, ages over ten years, or sits against a scar is physical, human knowledge.
  • Hold the needle. There is no AI on earth that has tattooed a single person. The craft — line weight, stretch, depth — is learned over thousands of hours on real skin.
  • Design for you instead of the average of everyone. Generic AI images are the average of a million other people's tattoos. A great artist makes yours unlike anyone else's.

So the deal is simple: InkBot gets your idea to the table; a human artist at Edison draws the final line. AI is the sketchbook, not the artist.

Why a mall shop in Nebraska built this

Because being inside Gateway Mall taught us something: foot traffic includes thousands of people who are tattoo-curious but tattoo-shy. They don't have "a guy." They don't know the vocabulary. They'd never cold-call a shop — but they will chat with a friendly AI on a website at midnight, and then walk into the mall on Saturday with an idea that's finally put together.

That's the whole strategy. Meet people where they are, lower the intimidation, then hand them to an artist who treats them like a person.
