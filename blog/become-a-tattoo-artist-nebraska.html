How to Become a Tattoo Artist in Nebraska (2026 Guide)

Every few weeks someone walks into Edison at Gateway Mall and asks a version of the same question: "I can draw — how do I do what you do?" This is the honest answer, for Nebraska specifically.

Step 1: Understand what Nebraska actually requires

Nebraska regulates body art through the Department of Health and Human Services. In practice, the requirements that matter to you are:

  • You can't legally tattoo out of your kitchen. Nebraska requires body art to happen in a licensed establishment with the proper setup — sanitation, sterilization, and recordkeeping. "Tattoo parties" and home studios aren't a gray area; they're illegal here.
  • Licensed shops carry the license; artists work within them. The shop's bloodborne-pathogen and sanitation standards are what keep clients (and you) safe, and every serious shop trains you in them.
  • Check the current rules with Nebraska DHHS before making plans — requirements get updated, and your shop will navigate most of the paperwork for you anyway.

The practical takeaway: your path runs through a legitimate shop, not around one.

Step 2: Build the portfolio that gets you an apprenticeship

An apprenticeship is how the craft actually transfers. Shops take apprentices seriously because we're investing hundreds of hours into you, so the portfolio is the audition. Here's what separates "has promise" from "next":

  • Draw every day. We can tell the difference between 40 pieces and 400.
  • Show tattoo-appropriate work: bold, clean lines that survive being put on skin. Delicate photorealism on paper is beautiful, but tattooing is its own medium — think in stencils, blackwork, and designs that fit body parts.
  • Include finished projects: a design series, flash sheets, a reworked "bad idea" tattoo into a good one. We want thinking, not just drafting.
  • Physical originals matter. A folder of digital drawings tells us less than a sketchbook you've carried for a year.

Step 3: Get the apprenticeship

Expect it to be long, humbling, and mostly cleaning. You'll start with station setup, sterilization, machine maintenance, and drawing for the artists before you ever touch skin. That's not hazing — that's the safety training that separates professionals from scratchers.

Questions to ask any shop offering you an apprenticeship:

  • Is it paid or unpaid, and for how long?
  • Who specifically is my mentor, and can I see their work?
  • What does the path to tattooing actual clients look like?
  • Will there be a contract? (There should be, protecting both sides.)

Be very wary of anyone who charges you a huge fee and promises you'll be tattooing clients in a month. That's how you learn to pay money for bad habits.

Step 4: The piercer path (it's different)

Piercing runs through its own training: bloodborne-pathogen certification, anatomy knowledge, placement and jewelry sizing skills, and a mentorship in sterile technique. Good piercers are in demand everywhere — including mall shops, which see more first-piercings than anywhere else. If you're people-first and detail-obsessed, piercing is a fantastic way into the industry.

Step 5: Apply to Edison (if you're in Lincoln)

We're Edison Inc., inside Gateway Mall at 6100 O St, Lincoln. What we look for when hiring — artists, piercers, and front-of-house:

  • Attitude before portfolio. We're a mall shop: you'll meet nervous first-timers, walk-ins, and families. Kindness is a job requirement, not a bonus.
  • A sketchbook or portfolio, digital or physical.
  • Reliability. Our hours run Mon–Sat 11–9, Sun 12–6. The shop runs on people who show up.

If that sounds like you, apply through our careers page: edisontattoo.net/careers. Bring your book to the shop if you'd rather show it in person — ask for the shop manager and say you're applying.

The honest timeline

From "I can draw" to "I'm a working tattoo artist" is typically 1–3 years: months of apprenticeship before you touch skin, then years of refining. There's no shortcut, and anyone selling one is selling you something. But if you love it, it's the best trade in the world — and Lincoln is a genuinely good town to build a career in it.
